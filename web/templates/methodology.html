The pipeline

  1. Claim intake. The input is normalised and validated. For articles, a check-worthiness scorer picks out statements that name entities, numbers or dates and avoids opinion and hedging.
  2. Structured records. Claims about capitals, heads of government and state, authorship, founding and birth years, and where a place is located are parsed and compared with Wikidata entity IDs and their current statements. If the record is ambiguous, FALXON abstains instead of guessing.
  3. Retrieval. Search queries are built from the subject phrase, named entities and the claim with negations removed, then sent to the Wikipedia search API. Up to {{ settings.max_pages }} articles are fetched at a pinned revision, split into single sentences and two-sentence windows.
  4. Reranking. {{ settings.rerank_model }}, a cross-encoder trained for passage relevance, scores every candidate against the claim. The best {{ settings.rerank_keep }} are kept, with at most three from any one article.
  5. Natural language inference. {{ settings.nli_model }}, trained on MNLI, FEVER-NLI and ANLI, reads the top {{ settings.nli_keep }} passages as premises and the claim as the hypothesis.
  6. Decision. A passage counts only if one label is both strong and well ahead of the others. Opposing strong passages yield Disputed; nothing strong yields Unverified. Thresholds are tuned on a separate FEVER split, never on the test set.

Known limitations

  • Only English Wikipedia and Wikidata are searched. Very recent events, local news, and claims about private people are usually out of reach.
  • Wikipedia is one publisher, so agreement across pages is not independent corroboration.
  • NLI models can be misled by scope (a state capital vs. a national capital), numbers, negation and sarcasm.
  • FEVER claims were written from 2017 Wikipedia; a few labels may have changed since.
  • “Confidence” is the model’s probability for the decisive passage, not a calibrated probability that the claim is true.
